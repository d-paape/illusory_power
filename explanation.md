Say we carry out an experiment with two experimental factors with two levels each. We are measuring the reading time on the final word.

A: John entered the church and met a barber.  
B: John entered the church and met a priest.  
C: John was walking around and met a barber.  
D: John was walking around and met a priest.

The first factor is CONTEXT, which is either constraining ('church') or unconstraining ('walking around'). The second factor is ENCOUNTER, which is expected ('priest') or unexpected ('barber'). In the unconstraining context, both encounters are assumed to be more or less equally expected. The hypothesis is that unexpected words create more processing difficulty, thus 'barber' should lead to longer reading times in condition A compared to 'priest' in condition B. Conditions C and D are control conditions to account for differences in the likelihood of encountering priests and barbers outside of a specific context.

We are predicting a **statistical interaction**: the difference in reading times between conditions A and B should be **bigger** than the difference between conditions C and D. In other words, the **difference of the two differences** should be significantly different from zero.

A mistake that is often made is to look at each of the differences separately, and argue for a significant interaction if one of the differences is significant and the other is not. For a true interaction, it is irrelevant whether the differences under comparison are themselves statistically significant: they are allowed to both be significant, but are also allowed to fail to reach significance. For the incorrect approach to testing 'interactions', however, one test has to fail and the other one has to succeed. The reasoning behind this second approach is fundamentally flawed, as the difference between significant and not significant is not necessarily statistically significant itself (Gelman and Stern, 2006): there may be an underlying difference between the other pair of conditions (C and D) that we failed to detect. How likely such detection failures (Type II errors) are depends on our statistical power, which in turn depends on the true difference in reading times between the conditions, our sample size, and the variability present in the data.

Note that even when the differences are the same, there is an increased probability of claiming an interaction even though none is really present when compared to the correct approach. The problem gets worse when the practice of arguing for such **imaginary interactions** is combined with **HARKing** ('hypothesizing after results are known'; Kerr, 1998): instead of making a prediction before analyzing the data ('the A-B difference should be bigger than the C-D difference'), one simply goes with whatever pattern of significance the data yield and adjusts the theory to match the result. This way, one would also argue for an interaction when the C-D comparison is significant but the A-B comparison is not, thereby increasing the chance of 'finding' an imaginary interaction.
